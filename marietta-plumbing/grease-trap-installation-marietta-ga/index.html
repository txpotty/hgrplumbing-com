    Expect roughly $1,500 to $4,000 for a small indoor trap and $7,000 to $20,000 or more for an outdoor gravity interceptor. A floor-recessed trap costs more than one set on the floor.     Watch the pump-out bills. Rising service frequency means the trap has been outgrown or the baffles have failed. Smell is another clue. Slow drains near the trap and failed inspections point to replacement too.

    That depends on the code your jurisdiction follows and the type of trap. Rules split, since hot water and detergent can carry grease past some indoor units. We check before we pipe it. Garbage disposers are usually not allowed to drain into a grease interceptor.

    You get the manufacturer's warranty on the unit and a written labor warranty from us on the installation. We register the unit with the maker so the factory warranty is on record. Keep your cleaning logs. Makers may ask for service records before paying a claim.

    An under-sink hydromechanical trap with good access is usually a half-day job, including the flow control and a leak test. Floor-recessed units take longer because we cut and patch concrete. Outdoor tanks are different. Plan on two to five days for a gravity interceptor once the permit is in hand, depending on depth, soil and how much paving we have to restore.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. Strictly, a grease trap is the small indoor unit rated in gallons per minute, and an interceptor is the large tank rated in gallons of capacity. Codes vary on the wording. Some plumbing codes call both of them interceptors and separate them as hydromechanical and gravity, so we use whatever terms your permit office uses.

    Size comes from flow, and flow comes from your fixtures. We measure each connected fixture and calculate the gallons per minute they can send at once. Gravity tanks use volume instead. Outdoor tanks get sized by the sewer authority's formula, which we run for you.
