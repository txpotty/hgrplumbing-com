    Cost depends on whether it is a clog or a construction job. Clearing a fixture or main line is a few hundred dollars at most. Jetting and sewer repairs cost more, and yard drains are priced by length and digging. We quote in writing first.

    Clearing a clog does not need a permit. Pipe repairs often require one. Replacing a section of sewer, adding a cleanout, or connecting a new drain to the sewer generally requires a permit and an inspection, and we handle that paperwork and schedule the inspector as part of the job.

    Some do. Kitchen lines in busy households benefit from a yearly jetting. Sewer laterals with a history of roots need cutting every 12 to 18 months, and older cast iron with heavy scale may need jetting every couple of years to keep it from narrowing back down to a trickle.

    Roots grow toward the water and nutrients inside sewer pipe. A hairline crack is enough. Once a root tip gets in through a cracked joint or loose seal, it grows into a mass that catches paper and grease, and clay and older concrete pipe with many joints are the most vulnerable.

    A typical French drain or downspout system takes one to three days. Larger projects run longer. Dry wells, long trenches or saw-cut concrete can push it toward a week.

    Simple downspout extensions are a reasonable DIY project. Trench drains take more skill. A French drain or dry well needs correct slope, the right fabric and stone, and an outlet that will not dump water on a neighbor, and digging without an 811 locate risks hitting a gas or power line.

    A single fixture drain usually takes about an hour. Plan on one to two hours for a main line. Camera work adds 30 to 60 minutes, and a buried or missing cleanout adds more.

    Store-bought cleaners rarely clear a real clog. They often make it worse. The chemicals sit in the trap, heat up and can damage older pipe or soften glue joints, and if the clog does not clear, the plumber now has to open a trap full of caustic liquid. A hand auger is the better tool.
